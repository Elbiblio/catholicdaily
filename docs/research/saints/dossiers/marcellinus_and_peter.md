## Uncertainties and conflicts

- Exact birth dates, ages, and death year are not established. References vary between 303 and 304, so the profile says early fourth century or Diocletianic period only and omits lifespan.
- Damasus wrote within the fourth century but reports what an executioner told him in boyhood. The testimony is early and specific, yet still second-hand and commemorative.
- Damasus does not identify a converted executioner. That familiar expansion is rejected.
- The later Passion contains roles and elaborate narrative. Priest and exorcist remain reliably received titles; conversions, healing, speeches, burial detail, and miracles are labeled later hagiography rather than established fact.
- Graffiti and archaeology demonstrate cult and place, but material evidence cannot confirm every element of either literary narrative.

## Pastoral safety notes

- State execution and concealment are evils inflicted on the martyrs, not goods that made them holy.
- No graphic description is used, and readers are never urged to seek persecution, expose themselves dramatically, or remain with an abuser or coercive authority.
- Faithful response may include escape, documentation, legal help, confidential reporting, trauma-informed care, and protection of children or other dependants.
- Communities should follow the endangered person's lead, avoid publishing identifying details, and never shame prudent safety planning as cowardice.

## Theological review

- Christ is the faithful witness and refuge; the martyrs participate in his fidelity without making violent death salvific in itself.
- Matthew 10 joins courage with wisdom and innocence, countering reckless martyr spirituality.
- Veneration depends on God's grace and ecclesial memory, not on embellishing uncertain details.
- The profile rejects coercion, retaliation, and romanticized suffering while maintaining a clear confession of martyr witness.
