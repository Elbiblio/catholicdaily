## Mission, martyrdom, and national-reception review

- Adalbert is both Prague's second bishop and its first native Czech or Bohemian bishop. These are compatible claims, not rival identities.
- The profile names papal and Polish political backing. It neither reduces mission to politics nor pretends that missionaries arrived without unequal power.
- Prussian people are not labeled primitive, obstinate, or collectively violent. The assembly's initial expulsion is distinguished from a later lethal attack.
- Murder is condemned without using martyrdom to erase host agency. The practice requires consent, cultural consultation, unconditional aid, and peaceful departure after refusal.
- The papal source formally supports patronage of Bohemia and Poland. The profile also names later state-forming reception and expressly denies exclusive nationalist ownership.
- Early vitae are close to the events and use companion testimony, but their constructed speeches, cultural bias, and literary motifs require caution. Sacred-grove, seven-spear, beheading, head, body-ransom, and precise motive stories do not appear as established fact.

## Theological review

Christ is the object of mission, while his method of sending disciples as peaceful guests governs the modern application. Martyrdom witnesses to fidelity and condemns killing; it does not sanctify reckless intrusion, cultural contempt, conditional aid, political expansion, or coercion. The profile respects the dignity and agency of Prussian hosts, distinguishes rejection from murder, and makes consent, disclosure, consultation, and consequence-free refusal concrete. Patronage serves shared prayer rather than nationalist possession.
