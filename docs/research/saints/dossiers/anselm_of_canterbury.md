## Biography and anecdote review

- Primary works establish Anselm's theological voice but do not independently verify every biographical scene later associated with him.
- The secure chronology from Aosta through Bec to Canterbury is retained. Childhood scenes and the dramatic forcing of episcopal insignia into his hand are omitted because they are not needed and carry a different evidentiary status.
- His two periods outside England are described within disputes, appeals, negotiation, and eventual settlements. Exile is not treated as automatic proof that every tactic or institutional claim was correct.

## Atonement and abuse-safeguard review

- Satisfaction is not silently translated into the Father inflicting a punishment on an unwilling Son. Anselm's own dialogue emphasizes Christ's freedom.
- The profile distinguishes Anselm's account from later penal-substitution theories and uses restoration and reconciliation carefully, without claiming modern restorative justice is simply identical to his medieval framework.
- Suffering is not praised as valuable in itself. Nothing in the profile asks a person to remain in abuse, accept another's coercion, or imitate Christ by surrendering consent.

## Content review

The factual pass checked dates, places, Benedictine formation, Bec offices, Canterbury appointment, royal disputes, two exiles, major works, and the relationship between primary texts and later interpretation. It rejected unsupported childhood precision, dramatized appointment anecdotes, over-simple political villains, uncertain canonization details, generic patronage, and unsourced symbols.

## Theological review

Christ remains the center of both inquiry and salvation. Reason serves a faith animated by love and prayer, and its conclusions remain accountable to evidence and correction. The atonement account emphasizes divine initiative, the Son's free self-gift, the seriousness of sin, and restored communion. It does not caricature the Father as cruel, confuse satisfaction with a later penal theory, glorify suffering, or use theology to excuse abuse. Ecclesial freedom is paired with accountable leadership and concern for people affected by institutional decisions.
