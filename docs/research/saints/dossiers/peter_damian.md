# Saint Peter Damian

## Identity resolution

- Stable ID: `peter_damian`
- Profile kind: individual
- Celebration IDs: `peter_damian`, `saint_peter_damian_bishop_and_doctor_of_the_church`
- Canonical name and aliases: Saint Peter Damian; Peter of Damian; Pier Damiani
- Feast date and calendar scope: February 21; optional memorial in the General Roman Calendar
- Identity conflicts resolved: The reviewed sources support birth around 1007, entry at Fonte Avellana around 1035, election as prior in 1043, appointment as Cardinal Bishop of Ostia in 1057, and death at Faenza in 1072. Exact childhood stories, an exact birth date, and generic patronage claims are not needed. Peter's reform writings include both careful theology and severe polemic; the profile does not turn his rhetoric or prolonged fasting into norms for present readers.

## Copyright and media decision

The biography, practices, questions, and prayer are original. No passage from Peter's letters, papal catechesis, or modern translation is reproduced. The profile names the subject and reception of severe polemic without copying slurs or graphic classifications. No image is included because file-specific authorship, source, and licensing were not researched.

## Content review

The factual review checked the approximate birth, education, Fonte Avellana chronology, election as prior, range of writings, 1057 Ostia appointment, legatine work, reform subjects, death at Faenza, feast, and Doctor title. It compared the Vatican synthesis with Stanford's peer-reviewed chronology, Jasper's recent study of Fonte Avellana, and the Catholic University of America Press critical-translation description. A separate historical control was used for Liber Gomorrhianus and Leo IX's qualified reception. The final text neither reduces Peter to his harshest tract nor hides that the tract used demeaning language and pressed disproportionate penalties. Generic patronage and unverified symbols were removed; the source-established Cross remains.

## Theological review

Christ crucified, not institutional purity or bodily suffering, is the center of the guide. Reform names wrongdoing, protects affected people, uses evidence and oversight, and leaves room for truthful conversion; it never licenses humiliation or silencing. Historical fasting is not proposed as a universal discipline, and the practice requires health, vocation, and competent guidance. Mercy does not weaken safeguarding or accountability. Peter's limitations are acknowledged without denying grace at work in his prayer, learning, and public service.
